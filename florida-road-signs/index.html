Florida Road Signs & Signals

A picture study guide to the traffic signs, signals, and pavement markings a student driver in Florida needs to know. Start with shapes and colors — they give you the meaning even before you read the words — then work through each group.

This guide follows Chapter 6, Traffic Controls, of the Official Florida Driver License Handbook (FLHSMV, rev. 08/2023), and uses standard MUTCD (Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices) sign designs. It is a study aid, not a substitute for the official handbook or Florida law.

1. Sign shapes

In the United States, the shape of a sign is a code of its own. Learn the shapes first and you can often tell what a sign is telling you even if you cannot read it yet — at dusk, in rain, or when you only see it from the back.

Octagon

Stop. In the United States, only the STOP sign uses this eight-sided shape, so you can recognize it even from the back or at night.

Triangle (point down)

Yield. The downward-pointing triangle is reserved for the YIELD sign.

Circle

Railroad crossing ahead (the yellow advance-warning sign). In Florida, official hurricane evacuation route signs are also circular.

Pentagon

School zone or school crossing. The five-sided shape tells you children may be nearby.

Diamond

Warning. A diamond tells you to watch for a condition ahead — a curve, merge, crossing, or other hazard.

Vertical rectangle

Regulatory. Upright rectangles set rules you must follow, such as speed limits, lane use, and turn restrictions.

Horizontal rectangle

Guide and information. Wide rectangles give directions, distances, exits, and many service messages.

Pennant

No passing zone. This sideways triangle is posted on the left side of the road at the start of a no-passing area.

Crossbuck (X)

Railroad crossing at the tracks. The X-shaped sign is your last warning at the crossing itself; treat it as a yield to trains.

You will also see shield-shaped route markers for Interstate, U.S., and Florida state highways. Those are guide signs, and they are in the route-marker section below.

2. Sign colors

Color is the other quick clue. A red sign is almost always about stopping or not doing something. Yellow warns you. Orange means work is happening. Green, blue, and brown help you find your way or a service.

Red

Stop, yield, do not enter, wrong way, or a prohibited movement. If you see red, you are being told to stop or not to do something.

Yellow

General warning. Something ahead needs your attention — a curve, a crossing, a merge, or another change in the road.

Fluorescent yellow-green

High-emphasis warning for school zones, pedestrians, and bicyclists. The bright color is meant to catch your eye.

Orange

Temporary traffic control in construction and maintenance work zones. Slow down and expect workers, cones, and lane shifts.

White (with black legend)

Regulatory signs that set limits and commands, such as speed limits, one-way arrows, and lane-use rules.

Black

Often the background of ONE WAY signs and some night-speed signs. Black and white together still mean a regulation.

Green

Guide and direction information: destinations, distances, street names, and exits.

Blue

Motorist services (gas, food, lodging, hospitals, rest areas) and some guidance, including disabled-parking signs.

Brown

Public parks, recreation, and places of cultural or historical interest.

Fluorescent pink (less common)

Incident management, such as a crash or emergency ahead. You will not see this as often as the other colors, but it still means “pay attention.”

3. Regulatory signs 28

Regulatory signs tell you what you must do, or must not do, and they are enforceable by law. Most are white with black wording or symbols. Stop and yield are the important red exceptions. A few warning-colored signs (the yellow “must turn” arrow and the no-passing pennant) are included here because that is how the Florida handbook groups them for students.

Stop

Come to a full stop before the stop line, crosswalk, or intersection, then go only when it is safe.

All-way stop

Every direction must stop; the first vehicle to arrive goes first, and if you arrive together, yield to the driver on your right.

Yield

Slow down and give the right-of-way to traffic and pedestrians; stop if you need to, then go when the way is clear.

Do not enter

Do not drive into this roadway — it is used for one-way or separated traffic coming toward you.

Wrong way

You are driving against traffic; turn around immediately and get into the correct direction.

Prohibition symbol (red circle and slash)

The red ring and slash over a symbol means you must not do what the sign shows.

No U-turn

You must not turn your vehicle all the way around to go the opposite direction.

No right turn

You must not turn right at this intersection.

No left turn

You must not turn left at this intersection.

No turn on red

After a red light, you may not turn; wait for a green signal.

No turns

You must not turn right or left here; continue straight only.

Must turn (two-direction arrow)

The road does not continue straight; you must turn left or right.

One way

Travel only in the direction of the arrow.

Keep right

Stay to the right of the island, median, or obstruction.

Slower traffic keep right

Stay in the right lane unless you are passing, and move right when a faster vehicle wants to go by.

No passing zone

This pennant, posted on the left side of the road, marks a stretch where you may not pass.

Pass with care

Passing is allowed again, but only when you can do it safely.

Speed limit

Do not drive faster than the posted number (this example shows 50 mph).

Exit speed

The fastest safe speed for an expressway exit ramp (this example shows 25 mph); slow down before you enter the ramp.

Speeding fines doubled

In an active school or work zone, a speeding ticket can cost twice as much.

Turn lanes

The arrows and the word ONLY tell you which movement each lane must make at the intersection ahead.

Center turn lane

The shared center lane is only for left turns from either direction — not for passing or through travel.

Left lane must turn left

Traffic in the left lane has to turn left at the next intersection (a matching sign is used for a right lane).

Begin right turn lane — yield to bikes

When you enter the right-turn lane, watch for bicyclists going straight and yield to them.

Restricted lane ahead

A lane ahead is reserved for certain vehicles, such as buses, carpools, or bicycles, often only at posted hours.

Emergency stopping only

You may stop here only for a true emergency, not to rest, make a call, or wait for someone.

No parking on pavement

If you stop, you must park completely off the paved roadway.

Parking by disabled permit only

Only a vehicle with an official disabled parking permit, carrying a person with a disability, may park here.

4. Warning signs 30

Warning signs are usually yellow and diamond-shaped. They do not give a new law by themselves; they tell you what to look out for so you can slow down, stay in your lane, or get ready to stop. An advisory speed plaque under a warning sign is the fastest safe speed for that condition — not the same thing as a legal speed limit.

Traffic signal ahead

A traffic light is coming up; slow down, especially if your view of the intersection is limited.

Stop sign ahead

A stop sign is ahead; start slowing down now and be ready to stop.

Yield ahead

A yield sign is ahead; slow down and be ready to stop or adjust your speed.

Speed reduction ahead

The speed limit is about to drop (this example shows 45 mph ahead); begin slowing before you reach the new limit.

Advisory speed

The fastest safe speed for the curve or condition ahead (often mounted under a warning sign). It is advice, not the legal speed limit.

Two-way traffic ahead

The road is about to carry traffic in both directions; keep right and watch for oncoming vehicles.

Merging traffic

Another lane of traffic joins yours from the right; watch for those vehicles and be ready to adjust your speed.

Lane reduction (right lane ends)

The right lane ends and traffic must merge left; if you are already in the left lane, make room when you can.

Roundabout ahead

A circular intersection is coming; slow down, yield to traffic already in the circle, and then enter.

Divided highway ahead

The road ahead splits, with a median or barrier between you and oncoming traffic.

Divided highway ends

The median is ending and two-way traffic will share the road; keep to the right.

Right curve

The road curves to the right; slow down and do not pass.

Sharp right turn

The road makes a sharp right turn; slow down more than you would for a gentle curve, and do not pass.

Reverse curve

The road curves one way and then the other; slow down and stay in your lane.

Winding road

Several curves are ahead; drive slowly and carefully and do not pass.

Cross road

Another road crosses yours ahead; look both ways for traffic entering the intersection.

Side road

A road enters from the direction shown (here, from the right); watch for traffic coming out.

Bicycle crossing

A bikeway crosses the road ahead; slow down and watch for bicyclists.

Emergency vehicle crossing

Watch for fire trucks or other emergency vehicles entering or crossing the road (often near a fire station).

Truck crossing

Watch for trucks entering or crossing the road, for example at a driveway, plant, or farm entrance.

Pedestrian crossing

People may be crossing the street; slow down and stop to yield to pedestrians.

Animal crossing

The animal on the sign is common here (this one is deer); watch especially at dawn, dusk, and night.

Low clearance

Do not go under this overpass or structure if your vehicle is taller than the height on the sign (this example is 12 feet 6 inches).

Pavement ends

The paved road changes to gravel or dirt ahead; slow down and watch your traction.

Soft shoulder

The ground beside the pavement is soft; stay on the pavement unless you have an emergency.

Slippery when wet

The road can be slick in rain; go slowly, avoid sudden braking or acceleration, and take turns gently.

Dip

There is a low place in the road; slow down and be ready to turn around if it is filled with water.

Narrow bridge

The bridge ahead has little extra width; stay centered in your lane and watch oncoming traffic.

One-lane bridge

The bridge is wide enough for only one vehicle at a time; wait if someone is already coming the other way.

Hill / downgrade

The road goes downhill ahead; slow down and be ready to shift to a lower gear to control your speed.

5. Guide signs and route markers 10

Green signs help you find your way: cities, streets, exits, and distances. Route shields tell you which highway you are on. A handy Florida rule: Interstates and U.S. routes that run east–west have even numbers, and north–south routes have odd numbers. On Florida Interstates, mile markers and exit numbers match.

Interstate route marker

The red, white, and blue shield marks an Interstate highway. In Florida you will often see I-4, I-10, I-75, and I-95. East–west routes have even numbers; north–south routes have odd numbers.

Interstate 4

I-4 is Florida’s main east–west Interstate across the peninsula, connecting Tampa, Orlando, and Daytona Beach.

Interstate 10

I-10 is the east–west Interstate across the Florida Panhandle, through Tallahassee and Jacksonville.

Interstate 75

I-75 is a major north–south Interstate through Florida, used by a lot of long-distance and local traffic.

U.S. route marker

The black-and-white shield marks a U.S. highway, such as US 1 along Florida’s east coast. These are usually not limited-access freeways.

Florida state road marker

The Florida outline shield marks a state road (this example is State Road 50). Follow these the same way you follow U.S. or Interstate numbers.

Destination / distance guide

Green signs tell you where a road goes and how far it is. This example shows Tampa, 2 miles ahead.

Exit gore / exit number

This green sign marks an interchange exit. On Florida Interstates, the exit number matches the mile marker.

Mile marker

Small green markers show your location on an Interstate in miles. In Florida they line up with the exit numbers, which helps if you need to describe where you are.

Hurricane evacuation route

Florida posts this circular sign on official evacuation routes. Follow it (and emergency instructions) if you are told to leave an area ahead of a storm.

6. Service and recreation signs 13

Blue-and-white signs point to driver services such as gas, food, lodging, hospitals, and rest areas. Brown-and-white signs point to parks, trails, beaches, and other recreation or historic places — very common on Florida trips.

Gas

A gas station is ahead or at the next exit.

Food

Food or restaurants are available ahead or at the next exit.

Lodging

A hotel or motel is ahead or at the next exit.

Hospital

A hospital is nearby. This sign is also used to point you toward emergency medical care.

Telephone

A public telephone is available ahead — still used at some rest areas and rural stops.

Rest area

A rest area is ahead (this example says 1 mile). These are official stopping places along major highways.

Accessible services

The wheelchair symbol marks facilities that are accessible to people with disabilities.

Camping

A campground is ahead or at the next exit.

Picnic area

A picnic area is nearby — a brown recreation sign rather than a blue service sign.

Hiking trail

A hiking or walking trail is nearby.

Swimming / beach

A swimming area or beach is nearby — a common recreation sign in Florida.

Wildlife viewing

A place to see wildlife is nearby; still drive carefully — animals may be near the road.

Recreation destination

Brown guide signs point to parks, scenic areas, and places of cultural or historic interest. This example directs you to Everglades National Park.

7. Work zone and construction 12

Orange diamonds, cones, drums, and barricades mean temporary conditions. Slow down, leave extra space, and follow flaggers, officers, and arrow boards. Speeding fines can be doubled in an active work zone. Florida also uses a “Prescribed burn ahead” sign when land managers are running a planned fire — watch for smoke.

Road work ahead

A work zone starts soon (this example says 1,000 feet). Slow down, stay alert, and watch for workers and changing lanes.

Flagger ahead

A person will be directing traffic; be ready to stop, slow, or shift lanes when the flagger signals you.

Workers ahead

People are working on or next to the road; slow down and give them plenty of room.

Prescribed burn ahead

A planned fire is being used to manage land nearby (common in Florida). Slow down, watch for smoke, and turn on headlights if visibility drops.

Right lane closed ahead

The right lane will be closed (this example says ½ mile); merge left as soon as you can do it safely.

Detour

The normal route is closed; follow this arrow (and later detour signs) to get around the work area.

End road work

You are leaving the work zone; return to the normal speed when the roadway and signs say it is safe to do so.

Traffic cone

An orange cone is a channelizing device that marks a temporary edge or closed area; do not drive over or between cones unless a lane is clearly open.

Drum (barrel)

Orange-and-white drums guide you through a work zone, especially at night; keep to the side they open toward.

Barricade

Striped barricades close or taper a lane. The stripes slope downward in the direction you should travel.

Flashing arrow panel

A lighted arrow board tells you to move out of a closed lane; follow the arrow and merge when it is safe.

Flagger paddles (STOP / SLOW)

A flagger may use a STOP or SLOW paddle (or a red flag) to hold you, slow you, or wave you through a work zone.

8. School zone signs 5

School signs are five-sided or use fluorescent yellow-green so they stand out. Watch for children at all times, not just during school hours. In Florida, a school zone is often 20 mph when the lights are flashing or during the posted times. Always obey a crossing guard, and remember the special stop rules when a school bus is stopped with its red lights flashing.

School

You are near a school. Watch for children at all hours, not only during the school day.

School crossing

Children may be crossing here; slow down, stop if you need to, and always obey a crossing guard.

Begin school zone (when flashing)

Do not go faster than the school-zone speed (often 20 mph in Florida) during the times shown or when the lights are flashing.

End school zone

The reduced school-zone speed ends here; return to the regular posted limit.

School bus stop ahead

A school-bus stop is just ahead, often hidden by a hill or curve; be ready to stop for the bus and for children.

9. Railroad crossing signs and signals 7

Slow down as you approach a highway-rail crossing and be ready to stop. Do not stop on the tracks or within 15 feet of the nearest rail. You must stop if the lights are flashing, the gate is down, a flagger is warning you, or a train is clearly coming. If there is more than one track, wait until every track is clear. Buses and many trucks must stop even when the lights are dark — leave them room.

Railroad advance warning

This round yellow sign is usually the first warning that a highway-rail crossing is ahead; slow down and be ready to stop.

Crossbuck (railroad crossing)

Treat the crossbuck like a yield sign: trains always have the right-of-way. Slow down, look and listen, and stop if a train is coming.

Number of tracks

A plaque under the crossbuck tells you how many tracks there are (this example is 3). After one train passes, look for another on a remaining track.

Flashing red lights and crossing gates

Stop as soon as the lights flash, before the gate comes down. It is illegal and very dangerous to drive around or under a gate, even if it is moving.

Railroad pavement marking

A large X and the letters R on the pavement mark the approach to a crossing; stay behind the stop line and never stop on the tracks.

Dynamic envelope markings

The painted X pattern shows the danger zone a train needs to pass through. Do not stop inside this area — stay at least 15 feet from the nearest rail.

Do not stop on tracks

Never stop your vehicle on the rails. If traffic is backed up, wait until you have room to clear the tracks completely.

10. Traffic signals 25

Traffic lights, arrows, lane signals, pedestrian signals, and Florida’s many drawbridge signals all control who goes and who waits. If a traffic light is out and no officer is directing traffic, treat the intersection as a four-way stop. Drivers, pedestrians, and bicyclists must obey signals unless an officer tells them otherwise.

Steady red light

Stop behind the stop line and wait for green. After a full stop, you may turn right (or left from a one-way street onto a one-way street) unless a sign says no — yield first to pedestrians and other traffic.

Steady yellow light

The light is about to turn red. Stop if you can do so safely; do not race through the intersection.

Steady green light

Go if the intersection is clear. Yield to pedestrians and to vehicles already in the intersection, and yield to oncoming traffic when you turn left.

Steady red arrow

Do not go in the direction of the arrow until it turns green. The same right-turn-after-stop rule can apply if no sign prohibits it.

Steady yellow arrow

The protected turn is ending; stop if you can do so safely.

Steady green arrow

You may turn in the direction of the arrow. Yield to people and vehicles still in the intersection.

Flashing yellow arrow

You may turn in the direction of the arrow after yielding — oncoming traffic has a green light.

Flashing red light

Treat it like a stop sign: come to a full stop, then go when it is safe.

Flashing yellow light

Proceed with caution. These are used at some intersections and to highlight a warning, such as a school crossing or a sharp curve.

Lane signal — red X

Do not drive in the lane under a red X. These signals are used for reversible lanes, toll booths, and lane closures.

Lane signal — yellow X

The lane is about to close or switch; prepare to leave it safely.

Lane signal — green arrow

You may use this lane, and you must still obey all other signs and signals.

Ramp meter — red

Ramp signals control how quickly cars enter an expressway. Stop and wait for green before you go.

Ramp meter — green

When the ramp signal turns green, one vehicle may enter the expressway; then wait for the next green if you are behind.

Walk / walking person

Pedestrians may start crossing the street in the crosswalk.

Don’t walk / raised hand

Do not start crossing. If you are already in the crosswalk when the hand starts flashing, finish crossing quickly.

Pedestrian countdown

The number counts down the seconds left to finish crossing. Once the countdown and flashing hand start, it is no longer safe to enter the crosswalk.

Rectangular rapid flashing beacon (RRFB)

A pedestrian can push the button to flash the yellow lights. Drivers must stop and yield to people in the crosswalk.

Pedestrian hybrid beacon (HAWK)

If the signal is dark, go if the way is clear. Flashing yellow: slow down. Steady yellow: prepare to stop. Steady red: stop for pedestrians. Flashing red: stop, then go if the crosswalk is clear.

Drawbridge warning

A drawbridge is ahead. Slow down and be ready to stop when the bridge opens for boats — a common situation on Florida waterways.

Drawbridge signal — red

Stop at the stop line. The bridge is open or moving, and the roadway is closed to cars and pedestrians.

Drawbridge signal — yellow

The signal is about to turn red and the bridge is about to operate; stop if you can do so safely.

Drawbridge signal — green

The bridge is down and you may go, still with caution.

Drawbridge flashing yellow

The drawbridge is in operation; slow down and prepare to stop.

Drawbridge gates

Stop when the lights flash and before the gate comes down. Never drive around or under a drawbridge gate.

11. Pavement markings 17

Lines, arrows, and words on the road work together with the signs. A simple rule of thumb: yellow lines separate opposite directions; white lines separate lanes going the same way, or mark the right edge and stop lines. Unless you are turning, exiting, changing lanes, or briefly moving left to pass a bicycle or pedestrian when it is safe, stay inside the lines of your lane.

Solid white edge line

Marks the right edge of the roadway. Stay to the left of this line except when you must leave the road safely.

Solid yellow edge line

Marks the left edge on a divided highway or one-way street.

Single broken white line

Separates lanes going in the same direction. You may cross it to change lanes when it is safe.

Single solid white line

You may travel in the same direction on both sides, but do not cross unless you must, for example to avoid a hazard. Often used to discourage lane changes near intersections.

Double solid white lines

Changing lanes across these lines is not allowed.

Single broken yellow line

Separates opposite directions. Stay to the right; you may cross to pass only when it is safe.

Double solid yellow lines

Do not pass or cross these lines (you may turn left or pass a bicyclist when it is safe).

Double yellow — solid on your side

Passing is not allowed from your lane. The other direction may have a broken line. You may still turn left or pass a bicyclist when safe.

Double yellow — broken on your side

Passing is allowed from your lane when it is safe; oncoming traffic may not pass.

Stop line and marked crosswalk

Stop before any part of your vehicle crosses the wide white stop line. Yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk — they have the right-of-way.

Turn-only arrow and ONLY

If your lane has a curved arrow and the word ONLY, you must turn in that direction.

Optional turn / through arrows

A curved arrow plus a straight arrow means you may turn or go straight from that lane.

Two-way left-turn lane (pavement)

Drivers from either direction may use the center lane only for left turns — never for passing or as a travel lane.

Reversible lane markings

Some highways reverse a lane at rush hour. The pavement, overhead lane signals, and signs work together — follow the signal over your lane.

Exclusive bicycle lane

A solid white line and a bicycle symbol reserve this lane for bikes. Motor vehicles may not drive in it; you may cross it to turn only when no bicyclist is there.

Shared-lane marking (sharrow)

The bicycle-and-chevron marking means bicyclists may use the full lane. It also shows them where to ride and discourages riding the wrong way.

Yellow curb and diagonal stripes

A yellow curb often means no parking (for example near a hydrant or intersection). Yellow or white diagonal stripes mark an obstruction or a keep-out area — do not park or drive through them, including fire lanes.
